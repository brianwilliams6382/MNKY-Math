# Expectancy Theory

<span class="mm-label">_[[def_neighbor-deep|Deep neighbor]]_</span>

## What it is  
  
**Expectancy Theory** is a theory of motivation that explains behavior through three linked judgments:

- **Expectancy** — If I put in the effort, can I perform successfully?
- **Instrumentality** — If I perform successfully, will the expected outcome actually follow?
- **Valence** — Do I value that outcome enough for it to matter?

The theory is most closely associated with organizational psychologist Victor Vroom, who introduced the model in _Work and Motivation_ in 1964. It is often called **VIE Theory** for valence, instrumentality, and expectancy.

The important feature is that motivation does not depend only on the existence of a reward.

The person has to believe the path between effort and outcome is credible, and the outcome itself has to matter to them.

> [!info] **In plain language: people are more likely to act when they believe their effort can work, believe success will lead somewhere, and care about where it leads.**

---
## Why it matters to MNKY Math  
  
Expectancy Theory matters to MNKY Math because it helps explain why the same consequence structure does not produce the same behavior in every person.

A system may offer a reward.<br>But that does not mean the person believes they can reach it.

A system may promise an outcome.<br>But that does not mean the person trusts the promise.

A system may create a consequence.<br>But that does not mean the consequence carries the same value for everyone.

This connects directly to how MNKY Math thinks about [[def_consequence-structure|consequence structures]].

➡️ The external structure may be identical.

➡️ The perceived structure may not be.

Two employees may face the same incentive and calculate very different paths because they differ in confidence, trust, history, perceived fairness, prior experience, personal values, or expectations about what the system will actually do.

Expectancy Theory matters because ==consequences shape behavior through perception, not merely through their existence.==

## Where we overlap  
  
Expectancy Theory and MNKY Math overlap around motivation, consequence, incentives, expectation, perceived likelihood, value, and decision-making.

Both recognize that behavior depends on more than the presence of a reward or penalty.

Both care about whether people believe:

- effort will matter
- performance is achievable
- the system will respond as promised
- the consequence is worth pursuing
- the cost of acting is justified
- the outcome carries enough value to motivate action

This makes Expectancy Theory especially useful when a system designer says:

> “But the incentive is there.”

MNKY Math asks whether the person experiencing the system sees the same pathway the designer sees.

---
## Where MNKY Math differs  
  
Expectancy Theory primarily explains motivation through the perceived relationship between effort, performance, and valued outcomes.

MNKY Math agrees, but extends the lens into how the system creates, teaches, damages, or modifies those expectations.

The question is not only: **Does the person expect their effort to produce a valued outcome?**

MNKY Math also asks:

> **_Where did that expectation come from?_**<br>**_What has the system previously taught the person about effort and reward?_**<br>**_What signals make the pathway feel credible or unreliable?_**<br>**_What happens when stated consequences differ from experienced consequences?_**<br>**_What personal history, trust, fear, bias, or prior disappointment shapes the expectation?_**<br>**_What alternatives does the person believe are available?_**<br>**_What happens when repeated experience teaches people that effort does not matter?_**<br>**_What behavior becomes rational once the consequence structure is no longer believed?_**

Expectancy Theory helps explain whether a motivational pathway makes sense to the person.

MNKY Math asks how the system built that pathway — and what happens when experience teaches people not to trust it.

---
## How it shows up  
  
Expectancy Theory appears wherever people decide whether effort is likely to be worthwhile.

- A salesperson ignores a bonus target because prior experience suggests the quota is unreachable regardless of effort.<br><br>
- An employee stops contributing ideas because previous suggestions produced no visible response, weakening the expectation that speaking up leads to change.<br><br>
- A student studies harder when they believe effort can improve performance and that improved performance leads to an outcome they care about.<br><br>
- A worker ignores a performance incentive because the reward matters less to them than schedule stability, autonomy, or reduced stress.<br><br>
- A customer stops responding to loyalty offers because previous rewards were difficult to redeem, weakening trust in the promised benefit.<br><br>
- A team disengages from a new initiative because earlier initiatives taught them that leadership attention will move elsewhere before the work matters.<br><br>

The system may still contain the stated incentive.

But the lived consequence structure has changed.

## MNKY Math lens

Expectancy Theory helps MNKY Math examine whether people believe the path between effort and outcome is real.

MNKY Math extends the lens by asking:

> - What effort is being requested?
> - What does the person believe that effort can produce?
> - What outcome has the system promised?
> - Does the person believe the system will actually deliver it?
> - How much does that outcome matter to this person?
> - What previous experience shaped those beliefs?
> - What competing consequences are present?
> - What happens when repeated experience contradicts the stated incentive?
> - What behavior does the person learn once the path loses credibility?

This is where incentive design becomes outcome credibility.

A system does not motivate simply because it attaches a reward.

1. The pathway has to be believable.
2. The outcome has to matter.

MNKY Math looks at both the cost of the path and the value of where it leads — asking what makes the “juice” matter, and what makes the “squeeze” worth enduring.
