# Plan or Promise? Where You Stand Matters

After sitting with [[plan-or-promise|the question]] a little longer, something else becomes hard to ignore.

The problem isn’t just that plans quietly turn into promises.

It’s that **the same plan is being interpreted on two different planets at the same time**.

And neither is wrong.


## Two Planets, One Company

Most organizations operate across at least two distinct altitudes.

At the top, success is experienced as **alignment, predictability, and coherence**. Plans function as stabilizing signals. Internally and externally, plans reassure boards, investors, and leadership teams that the system is under control, that commitments are being managed, that uncertainty is bounded.

At the ground level, success is experienced very differently. It looks like **adaptability, effort, and output under pressure**. New constraints appear early. Supply shifts, customer behavior changes, tools strain, people burn out. Reality moves before language does.

Both groups are working from the same plan.

They are not working from the same conditions.


## When Adjustment Means Different Things

From above, changing a plan can feel like introducing instability. It risks signaling that something promised is now in question — even if no promise was ever explicitly made.

From below, not changing the plan can feel irrational. New information is glaringly visible. The cost of pretending otherwise is accumulating in workarounds, delays, and quiet improvisation.

So a familiar tension forms.

Adjustments are framed upward ↗️ as exceptions.

Adaptations are framed downward ↘️ as necessities.

The plan remains intact on paper, while execution quietly diverges behind the scenes.


## Planning as a Stage Prop

This is where the plan begins to serve two roles at once.

- On one planet, it remains a symbol of alignment.
- On the other, it becomes a prop — maintained for optics while real work happens elsewhere.

> Everyone knows this is happening.

No one quite says it out loud.

By the time the divergence becomes visible, the language around the plan has already hardened. What began as intent is now treated as commitment. What began as direction is now measured as delivery.

The promise wasn’t spoken… but it is being enforced.


## Familiar Patterns, Different Altitudes

You’ve seen this play out.

In annual plans that feel stable at creation, then strain under new conditions months later. Reconciliation becomes a negotiation between what was said and what became possible.

In work that begins with exploration, discovery, and learning — only to later be evaluated as if uncertainty should have already been resolved. The same initiative moves from _“we’re figuring this out”_ to _“why didn’t we know this sooner,”_ without a clear handoff between the two.

Different phases. Different definitions of success.

One continuous story.


## Where Metrics Step In

This is where metrics quietly take on more power than intended.

Metrics are meant to translate intent into action. Metrics connect strategic language at the top with operational work at the ground. They are supposed to help both planets see the same thing.

But when conditions change and metrics don’t, something subtle happens.

Metrics begin to **ossify**.

> They stop functioning as indicators of learning and start behaving like enforcers of past intent. What was once directional becomes declarative. What was once provisional becomes binding.

At that point, both planets are trapped.

- Leadership feels constrained by promises they didn’t knowingly make.
- Operators feel constrained by measures that no longer reflect reality.

The plan holds.

The system strains.


## No Villains, Just Gravity

What makes this pattern persistent is that it doesn’t rely on bad actors.

Executives aren’t ignoring reality.

Operators aren’t resisting alignment.

They are responding to different gravitational forces, shaped by different definitions of success, risk, and responsibility.

The plan didn’t fail.

It just couldn’t survive unchanged across two planets.


## Seeing the split is the shift to hang with

This isn’t a call to abandon planning.<br>It isn’t an argument against metrics, structure, or discipline.

It’s a recognition that many of the tensions organizations experience aren’t caused by incompetence or deception — but by unacknowledged perspective splits.

Plans turn into promises not only because of language, but because different parts of the system need them to mean different things.

Once you see the planets, the confusion starts to make sense.

**And once it makes sense, it becomes much harder to pretend it isn’t there.**
