# When Repeating Becomes Routine

You step into the shower.

Without thinking much about it, you probably begin doing things in roughly the same order you did yesterday.

And the day before that.

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;">❍ Maybe shampoo comes before soap.<br>❍ Maybe your face gets washed before anything else.<br>❍ Maybe you always turn around the same direction.</div>

You probably have a sequence.

You may just have never thought of it as one.

➖

The same thing may happen when you get dressed.

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;">Which leg goes into your pants first?<br><br>❍ Left?<br>❍ Right?<br><br>Do you know?<br><br>If you do, try the other leg next time.<br><br>Don't just notice what you do.<br>Notice what happens when you deliberately do it differently.<br><br>Does it require more thought?<br>Feel awkward?<br><br>Try this over a handful of days. How quickly does your body try to return to the familiar sequence? Or does it willingly adapt to your new sequence?</div>

➖

What about when you arrive somewhere familiar?

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;">🗹 You may park in roughly the same area.<br>🗹 Walk through the same entrance.<br>🗹 Put your things in the same place.<br>🗹 Take roughly the same path toward whatever comes next.</div>

None of this seems particularly interesting.

Until something changes.

⛔ The shampoo isn't where it belongs.<br>⛔ Someone parked in _your_ spot.<br>⛔ The entrance you normally use is locked.

Suddenly something that required almost no attention...

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;">requires your attention.</div>

Which raises two questions: Why does repetition come so naturally? And what is it doing for us?

---
## Repetition Changes the Experience

Your day is typically filled with hundreds of ordinary acts, many mirroring the days that came before.

Some may exist through direct intention:

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem; font-style: italic; color: var(--darkgraymm)">This is where I get my coffee.</div>

But many simply found their way into your life as _you_:

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem; font-style: italic; color: var(--darkgraymm)">This is how I butter my toast.</div>

Some things are important enough that we choose to repeat them.

But something doesn't have to be important for it to be repeated.

Every repetition has a beginning, chosen or not.

Consider for a moment what happens when you encounter a new building or structure.

-> Maybe its day one at a new job.<br>-> Or you're moving into a new apartment or house.<br>-> Or maybe you're vacationing at a new resort.

At first, almost everything asks something of you. The first time you enter the unfamiliar building, you may have to look around.

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem; font-style: italic;">Where do I go?<br>Which door?<br>Where's the elevator?<br>What floor?</div>

By the tenth time, there's less to figure out.

Eventually you may walk through the same building while thinking about something else entirely.

The building didn't necessarily become simpler.

**Your relationship with it changed.**

Your [[def_interior-systems|interior system]] has encountered these conditions before.

It has something to work from.

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;">⏵ The path is familiar.<br>⏵ Some uncertainty has disappeared.<br>⏵ Some decisions no longer need to be remade.<br>⏵ Some things no longer require the same amount of conscious attention.</div>

What was once an experience to navigate, then repeated, can quietly become a routine.

---
## You Don't Have to Design One

That's one of the more interesting things about routine.

You don't necessarily sit down and decide to create it.

You probably never declared:

> Beginning today, I shall henceforth insert my left leg first.

You did something.<br>Then encountered similar conditions again.<br>And did something similar.

Again.<br>Again.<br>Again.

Eventually a recognizable sequence existed.

Your interior system doesn't have to treat every familiar encounter as an entirely new problem. You do something once. Your interior system learns something from it. You reencounter similar conditions. Memory contributes to what happens next. The action or response may repeat.

Repetition gives your interior system something it has seen before. Something it can anticipate. 

Over time, familiar sequences can begin asking less of your active attention, whether you are intentional about engaging repetition or simply moving about your day with memory and recall doing their jobs. 

And that's more consequential than simply making the sequence easier or more efficient.

Routine can mean that part of your attention becomes available for something else.

- Something different.
- Something unexpected.
- Something that may matter more.

Routine doesn't just help us repeat. It can help create room to notice what is outside of the routine.

One way it does that is by turning many separate decisions and movements into something your interior system can increasingly handle as one familiar sequence.

As those pieces merge, their separateness can begin fading from awareness.

Think about a child learning to tie a shoe.

At first, almost every movement matters.

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;">Which lace goes where?<br>What crosses what?<br>Where does the loop come from?</div>

The child attempts.

Each step is still mostly its own thing.

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;">Tries again.<br>Remembers enough to try something similar.<br>Adjusts.<br>Repeats.</div>

Over repeated attempts, some of those separate movements begin merging into a single fluid action.

What once required several small acts of attention begins requiring fewer.

Eventually, what began as several separate steps starts being experienced more like one thing: tying a shoe.

➖

Now think about teaching a child to tie their shoe.

Do you suddenly feel the effort of turning that _one thing_ back into all its separate little steps?

The steps didn't disappear. Your need to see them separately did.

This [[def_automaticity|automaticity]] means your interior system has learned something it can reuse. Sometimes so well that the many little steps fade into one familiar action.

Repetition can change how much work a familiar sequence asks of you.

But it can also change what your interior system comes to treat as baseline.

---
## Familiar Doesn't Mean Invisible

When separate steps fade or merge and routine begins creating expectations, something else starts taking shape:

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;"><strong>a baseline.</strong></div>

A baseline is what your interior system has learned to expect under familiar conditions.

Baselines can function a little like [[math-is-how-we-try-to-make-sense-of-systems|metrics]]. They reduce something complex into a reference against which difference becomes easier to detect.

This isn't necessarily conscious. Your interior system is continually using prior experience, memory, context, and expectation to help interpret what it encounters.

- Familiar route
- Familiar sound
- Familiar sequence
- Familiar person in a familiar place

Familiar doesn't have to disappear.

> **It simply doesn't have to be solved again from scratch.**

From an evolutionary perspective, starting over every time would be extraordinarily expensive in time, energy, and attention.

And repetition and recurrence are not just quirky features of being human. 

Step back far enough and repetition starts showing up almost everywhere, like something that is deeply woven through all living systems.

Biological rhythms repeat within cells. Even some single-celled organisms can change how they respond after repeated exposure. Across species, memory and learning allow familiar routes, signals, and responses to influence what happens when similar conditions return.

The mechanisms are different. So are their purposes.

Yet across scales, something striking keeps appearing:

> **Life rarely behaves as though nothing has happened before.**

Something gets carried forward.

For organisms capable of learning, the advantage is similar: _experience participates in what happens next._

When the familiar requires less figuring out, more attention can be available for what differs.

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;">-> Something unexpected<br>-> An opportunity<br>-> Food<br>-> A potential mate<br>-> A possible threat</div>

**_A baseline is therefore a kind of shorthand that makes difference easier to see._**

Walk into a building you've never visited and you may not know the layout.

But you probably already know how to look for the entrance, signage, service counters, queues, and other familiar signals.

**Novel doesn't mean baseline-free.**

Your interior system can compare what it encounters with expectations carried in from other places.

_**Attention isn't ever starting from zero.**_

We enter new environments carrying expectations learned in old ones.

Visit or work inside that building every day and much of what first required attention can become background.

- Until something isn't where it belongs.
- Something sounds different.
- A familiar process stops behaving familiarly.

Then attention returns.

You may barely notice the baseline until something deviates from it.

_Because_ your interior system already had something to compare the present against...

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;">that difference can get through.</div>

And the same repetition that gives your interior system a baseline can give the systems around you one too.

---
## The routine to hang with

Routine can look ordinary because it usually is.

- A shower
- Getting dressed
- Parking
- Walking into work
- Making coffee
- Opening an app

But ordinary doesn't mean insignificant. 

Repetition can quietly change how much attention familiar participation asks of you.

- What once required attention can require less.
- What once required a decision may stop feeling like one.
- What once felt unfamiliar can become expected.

Repeated long enough, a learned way of participating can stop feeling learned.

And eventually, something that once required attention may simply feel like **==you==**.

Which raises a couple of uncomfortable questions...

**How much of it is really _you_?**

And...

**_Who benefits from the routine?_**

Because the same repetition that helps your interior system move through the familiar with less active attention can also make your participation more familiar to the systems around you.

And that creates a doorway. 

**Something the systems around you can use.**<br><div style="margin-left: 1.7rem;"><strong>Too.</strong></div>
