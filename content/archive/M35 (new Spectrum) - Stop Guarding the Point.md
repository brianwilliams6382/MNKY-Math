# Stop Guarding the Point

Pick something you feel strongly about.

Not something you merely prefer.

Something where you have a position.

Maybe you've argued for it. Defended it. Explained why another way doesn't make sense. Maybe you've never had to defend it at all because, to you, it just seems obvious.

Hold that point for a moment.

Now ask yourself whether someone else could reasonably hold a different position.

If the answer is no, pause.

You may be dealing with something closer to a factual claim than a position. Or perhaps the disagreement doesn't sit inside the fact itself, but somewhere around what the fact means, what caused it, how important it is, or what should happen because of it.

If there's genuinely nowhere else to stand, choose something else.

We need at least two points.

Now take the position that feels most opposed to yours.

Put yours on one side.

Put that one on the other.

Those are your goalposts.

For now.

Between them, find another position.

Not a compromise. Not the average.

Just something that could meaningfully exist between the two.

Describe it.

Now look outside your original goalposts.

Can you imagine a position beyond yours?

What about beyond the opposing position?

If you can, move the posts.

Then do it again.

Find something between one of your new points and one that was already there.

Maybe another.

Maybe one more.

Then stop.

Look at what you made.

You started with a point.

Then an opposing point.

Then something between them.

Then something beyond them.

Eventually, you produced a field.

And somewhere inside that field sits the thing that originally felt like one end of the argument.

## You constructed it

You didn't discover **the** spectrum.

You constructed **a** spectrum.

That distinction matters.

The first two positions felt like endpoints because they were the first boundaries you chose.

But the goalposts were choices.

Move them and the field changes.

Add another dimension and the field may change again.

Change the conditions and some of the positions may move closer together, farther apart, or stop being particularly useful ways of describing the issue at all.

This doesn't mean the positions are imaginary.

It means the model you created to arrange them is doing exactly what models do.

It's helping make variation easier to see.

The field is not the thing.

It's one way of seeing the thing.

## The same points can create different fields

Now imagine two people perform exactly the same exercise.

Oddly enough, they both arrive at the same five labels:

A — B — C — D — E

That looks like remarkable agreement.

But one person experiences the relationship between those positions more like:

A——B—C————D—E

while the other sees:

A—B————C—D——E

Same labels.

Same order.

Different geometry.

For one person, B and C may feel almost indistinguishable while the distance to D feels enormous.

For the other, B and C may represent the biggest divide on the entire field.

And neither person has necessarily changed what A, B, C, D, or E are called.

**Agreement on the labels does not mean agreement on the geometry.**

That becomes even more interesting when conditions change.

Maybe something that mattered very little suddenly carries more weight.

The positions don't necessarily have to change.

Their relationship might.

A——B—C————D—E

can become:

A—B————C—D——E

without any of the labels changing at all.

Which means two people can appear to be discussing the same spectrum while actually navigating rather different fields.

## Your interior system helped build it

Why did you choose those original endpoints?

Why did one position feel moderate?

Why did another feel extreme?

Why did something seem close to your own position while something else felt impossibly far away?

Those judgments didn't arrive from nowhere.

Your history was present.

Your experience.

What you've learned.

What you've trusted and distrusted.

What has worked for you.

What has hurt you.

Who you listen to.

What you fear losing.

What you hope to preserve.

Even status may have participated by changing how much weight you gave one source, argument, or person compared with another.

Your interior system didn't merely observe the points.

It participated in arranging them.

Someone else brings a different interior system.

So even if they use the same words, recognize the same positions, and appear to be looking at the same field...

they may not be.

## And then the conditions move

Suppose nothing about the five positions changes.

But the stakes do.

Or time becomes scarce.

Or money enters the picture.

Or safety.

Or belonging.

Or responsibility for someone other than yourself.

Suddenly a position that once seemed remote may move closer.

Something that looked reasonable in one condition may become unreasonable in another.

Or the entire line may stop making sense.

Sometimes the problem isn't where something belongs on the spectrum.

**Sometimes we're using the wrong spectrum.**

Maybe what looked like a single line actually requires two dimensions.

Maybe the endpoints were defined around the wrong variable.

Maybe a new condition creates a different set of goalposts entirely.

The field isn't fixed simply because we drew it.

## Seeing more doesn't make everything equal

There is an easy mistake available here.

If there are positions between two points, and positions beyond those positions, perhaps every point deserves equal standing.

It doesn't.

A wider field can contain claims supported by strong evidence and claims supported by almost none.

It can contain positions that are ethical, harmful, useful, incoherent, practical, impossible, or simply wrong.

Seeing the field doesn't require surrendering judgment.

And it doesn't mean abandoning the point you started with.

Your original position may still be the one you believe is best.

Perhaps even more strongly than before.

The difference is that now you can see more of what surrounds it.

## Why MNKY Math tends to resist prescription

This is one reason MNKY Math tries hard not to become overly prescriptive.

Prescription can quietly assume more agreement about the field than actually exists.

Telling someone where they should move becomes considerably harder when you don't yet know what their endpoints are, how they space the positions between them, what variables they are weighting, what conditions surround the decision, or whether the two of you are even working from the same field.

That doesn't make prescription useless.

Sometimes someone knows more.

Sometimes expertise deserves weight.

Sometimes a direction really is better supported than another.

But orientation probably deserves to come first.

Otherwise, it becomes remarkably easy to mistake your map for the territory.

## The field to hang with

Maybe the most interesting question isn't:

_Where do you stand?_

It might be:

_What did you have to construct for that place to become where you stand?_

Who placed the goalposts?

What made those positions feel like the extremes?

What sits between them?

What exists beyond them?

What happens when the conditions change?

And when another person appears to be standing somewhere else...

are you actually disagreeing about where something belongs?

Or are you standing inside different fields?

A point asks you to defend where you stand.

A field asks you to understand what surrounds it.

Maybe that's why we so often guard the point.

**The field asks more of us.**

You don't have to abandon the point to see the field.

But once you've seen the field, you may understand your point differently.

# ==🔴Thought for new to hang with==

By seeing the spectrum you can better see not just what you are choosing and not choosing, but how what you're choosing is influencing what you are accepting and rejecting, what you can and cannot see. And perhaps even more importantly, you can begin to see how the focus on this choice or point effects other points, meaning the impact the choice or position has on other points. Because a choice or a position may feel singular but it is rarely ever one thing. There is always a [[def_collateral-effect|collateral effect]] and quite often many. These become visible when you can better see the spectrum. And this information can help you to better tune the choice.

### The research support is very strong

There are several neighboring research traditions here, but four seem especially relevant.

**Bias blind spot** is almost exactly the self/other asymmetry you're describing. Emily Pronin and colleagues found that people are generally much better at recognizing bias as something affecting _other people_ than recognizing its operation in themselves. Pronin's later review specifically describes people as tending to recognize bias in human judgment except when assessing their own—and links part of that problem to our reliance on introspection even though many biases operate without conscious awareness. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17129749/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That gives research footing to your intuition that:

> _the well is much fuller when explaining the flaws in someone else's reasoning than when applying the same lens inward._

Not those exact words as a scientific claim, but the underlying asymmetry is well supported.

**Biased assimilation** is also highly relevant. Lord, Ross, and Lepper's classic 1979 study found that people with opposing prior positions evaluated mixed evidence differently, tending to judge evidence congenial to their existing view more favorably. Importantly, subsequent research has complicated how routinely exposure to mixed evidence actually causes _polarization_, so I would not make the stronger claim that contrary evidence generally makes people more extreme. But the central idea—that prior beliefs can influence how evidence itself gets evaluated—is highly relevant here. [Stanford University](https://web.stanford.edu/~mrosenfe/Soc150_syllabus.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Belief perseverance** gets even closer to your “data arrives but the person remains” condition. Anderson, Lepper, and Ross studied how explanations and beliefs could persist after the original information supporting them had been discredited. [CASBS](https://casbs.stanford.edu/people/mark-r-lepper?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

And wonderfully, there's research that supports the _structure of your exercise itself_: Lord, Lepper, and Preston's **“consider the opposite”** experiments found that deliberately generating possibilities contrary to one's current beliefs reduced biased assimilation and biased hypothesis testing more effectively than simply telling people to be fair and unbiased. [Stanford Profiles](https://profiles.stanford.edu/mark-lepper?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That last one feels particularly important for M35 because your exercise isn't:

> _Try not to be biased._

It's:

> **Generate another point. Now another. Now go beyond the endpoint. Now explain someone standing somewhere else.**

You're changing what the person has available to think with.

### I would make one important change to the premise

I wouldn't use **“unquestionable facts”** or necessarily even “data that suggests they should move.”

The interesting machinery becomes easier to defend if the essay says something closer to:

> **credible evidence appears that meaningfully challenges their position**

because evidence does not always require movement. The person may have other evidence you don't know about. The new data may be weak. It may change probability without reversing the conclusion. Different values can produce different decisions even with agreement on the facts.

And that complication actually helps the essay.

Because another question becomes:

> **Who decided how far this evidence should move the point?**

That is itself part of the field.

The essay isn't teaching readers that rational people obediently move whenever somebody presents data. It's teaching them to inspect **how evidence gets weighted**.

### This could deepen what “seeing the field” ultimately means

Originally, M35 had a very strong insight:

> You don't have to abandon the point to see the field.

I think this new exercise adds another layer:

> **Seeing the field also means becoming capable of seeing your own point from somewhere else in it.**

That may be one of the deepest benefits of the whole exercise.

You aren't merely becoming more tolerant of alternative positions.

You're acquiring enough distance from your own position to ask:

_Why does this point have the weight it has for me?_  
_What would make me move?_  
_What evidence am I discounting?_  
_What does remaining here protect?_  
_What would moving cost?_  
_Would I recognize the same reasoning as reasonable if it came from someone standing somewhere else?_

That is much more substantial than generic “open-mindedness.”

And I think there is an especially good final inversion hiding here:

> Earlier, you were asked to explain why someone else wouldn't move.
> 
> **How easily did the explanations come?**
> 
> Now you're standing on the point.
> 
> **What becomes harder to see?**

I wouldn't necessarily make that the essay's final close—we already have strong point/field language for that—but I could absolutely see it being the late-stage **Challenge** or the final return to the thought experiment immediately before _The field to hang with_.
