# The Coupon Paradox

Most of us have had the same small moment of frustration.

You’re standing at the register or reviewing an online receipt and realize once again that a coupon or savings existed. It was valid. It applied to something you bought. And yet, somehow, it didn’t make it into the transaction.

Nothing dramatic happened.<br>
No error message.<br>
No warning.<br>

Just a quiet sense that the system worked exactly as designed… just not in your favor.

This is the coupon paradox.

## Digital Ease, Selective Friction

In theory, digital tools should make this problem disappear.

Sellers know who we are.<br>
They know what we buy.<br>
They know which discounts apply.

From a purely technical standpoint, many systems could automatically apply eligible offers without us lifting a finger. And yet, many don’t.

Instead, they preserve a small but meaningful step: **_Selection_**

1. You must find the coupon.

2. You must activate it or present it.

3. You must remember to do so at the right moment.

If you don’t, the system moves on (quietly pocketing the difference).

## When Friction Isn’t Accidental

This is where “breakage” enters the conversation.

Breakage is the value of rewards, credits, or coupons that are issued but are never redeemed. In many business models, it’s not an anomaly. It’s expected. Sometimes it’s even built into forecasts. Originally, coupons were a blunt instrument, mass-distributed incentives meant to stimulate trial and visibility more than redemption. But redemption also produced something advertising otherwise struggled to provide: a trace connecting the promotion to a response — an early form of [[ref_advertising-measurement-evolution|advertising attribution]].

In a world of limited data, low redemption was a feature, not a flaw.

That’s the uncomfortable part.

Because breakage in today's world isn’t always the result of poor design. Often, it’s the result of intentional restraint: **_Systems that could remove friction choose not to._**

To be fair, this isn’t always exploitative.

Some retailers have moved in a more thoughtful direction by removing the friction of discovery while retaining the moment of choice. Coupons are surfaced prominently, aligned with recent purchases, and made easy to activate. The customer still selects, but no longer has to hunt.

In those cases, selection isn’t a trap: **_It’s a signal of intent._**

## The Quiet Assumption Beneath It All

The assumption behind many loyalty designs is subtle:

-> Requiring action deepens engagement<br>-> Selection establishes commitment<br>-> Effort strengthens loyalty

Sometimes that’s true.

But for everyday purchases, the unglamorous defaults like detergent, paper towels, or toothpaste, loyalty often works differently.

It’s built on predictability.<br>On reliability.<br>On relationship.

On knowing, or at least feeling, that the system isn’t waiting for you to slip.

When multiple sellers offer similar prices and availability, convenience tends to win. Over time, the place that requires the least vigilance becomes the default. Not because it demanded attention, but because it earned trust, made things easier, or made a good-enough outcome more predictable.

## Where the Paradox Lives

The real paradox isn’t simply that systems require customers to act.

It’s that many systems still profit from missed intent — while also training customers to expect the very mechanics that make intent easier to miss.

Coupons, promotions, loyalty programs, and rotating discounts can begin as ways to attract attention, differentiate from competitors, or protect market share.

And for some customers, the hunt becomes part of the value.

Finding the coupon.<br>Stacking the offer.<br>Waiting for the sale.<br>Knowing you paid less than someone else might have.

For others, the same mechanics become friction.

Another thing to remember.<br>Another price to compare.<br>Another reason to wonder whether today’s price is actually the price worth paying.

That creates a different kind of trap for the seller: institutional memory.

The promotion is no longer just a tactic. It may now be embedded in forecasts, pricing assumptions, customer expectations, operating routines, and what the organization has learned to believe works. What once began as a moat used to protect against competition can eventually become a fence that keeps the seller inside.

Customers learn to wait.<br>To search.<br>To redeem.<br>To distrust the posted price.<br>Or to believe that buying without the promotion means losing.

Removing the friction may improve the experience for one customer while taking something away from another.

And reducing the promotion may threaten behavior the system itself spent years teaching.

Digital tools are capable of recognizing what customers consistently do. They already see patterns. The question is not simply whether a system can use that knowledge to reduce effort or monetize it. It is also whether the system can still afford to change the behavior it helped create.

==And that choice may say more about a system’s priorities — and its constraints — than any loyalty slogan ever could.==

## A Quiet Contrast

Some systems ask us to prove our loyalty at each transaction.

Others recognize it once. And then remember us each time.

In our opening scenario the coupon did its job. It got your attention. Perhaps it even influenced what you purchased.

But when the savings didn't materialize, whose failure did it feel like?

You forgot. You missed the date. You didn't follow the conditions.

And perhaps all of those things are true.

But what if the system was designed around the likelihood that some people would?

The seller may have kept the benefit of your attention while you carried the cost of the outcome — and perhaps even the responsibility for who owns the failure.
