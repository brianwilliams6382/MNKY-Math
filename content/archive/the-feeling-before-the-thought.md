# The Feeling Before the Thought

Why do you like the music you like?

Why does one food feel comforting and another disgusting?

Why does one person love roller coasters while another wants absolutely nothing to do with them?

Why does a particular tone of voice put you immediately on edge? Or cause you to smile?

Why does one person feel most like themselves dressed to-the-nines, while another feels most like themselves when simply dressed?

You can probably explain some of these.

**_But where did your explanation come from?_**

More importantly...

> **Did the explanation come before the response?**

---
## Something Happens in Between

- A door closes
- A deadline moves closer
- Someone raises their voice
- A notification appears
- A person you trust enters the room
- A metric turns red

These are conditions.

But conditions don't arrive with meaning already attached.

They encounter an [[def_interior-systems|interior system]].

> And somewhere inside that interaction...
>
> meaning appears.

The deadline may register as motivating.<br>Or threatening.

The closed door may mean privacy.<br>Or trouble.

A raised voice may barely register for one person...<br>and completely change the experience for another.

Same external condition.

👉 Different interior system.

👉 Different first response.

---
## Interpretation Doesn't Always Wait for You

We tend to imagine interpretation as something deliberate.

1. Observe
2. Consider
3. Decide what it means. 

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;">Period.</div>

Sometimes it works that way.

But not always.

Daniel Kahneman offered a useful way of thinking about this in _Thinking, Fast and Slow_.

He described two broad modes of thinking.

- One is fast, automatic, associative, and requires little deliberate effort.
- The other is slower, more effortful, and more consciously analytical.

These aren't two literal parts of the brain.

They're a model for something most of us already recognize.

Sometimes we think our way toward an interpretation.

Other times...

the interpretation feels already baked in. Obvious. So we move on.

Consider a simple sentence:

> **_"Can I talk to you for a minute?"_**

For one person...<br>almost nothing happens.

For another: _What did I do?_

Same words.

Different interpretation.

The difference may come from what happened yesterday.

-> Or what happened in another job ten years ago.<br>-> Or who is asking.<br>-> Or the tone.<br>-> Or what kind of day you're already having.

By the time conscious thought starts assembling an explanation...

your interior system may already have responded.

---
## Thinking Longer Doesn't Always Mean Thinking Differently

This is where Kahneman's distinction gets more interesting.

It's tempting to imagine fast thinking as the biased part...<br>and slow thinking as the part that comes along and corrects it.

But slower thinking doesn't guarantee a new interpretation.

-> Sometimes we reason our way toward something different.<br>-> Sometimes we simply build a stronger explanation for where we already landed.

The first interpretation becomes the starting point.

Then we gather evidence.<br>Build a story.<br>Make it coherent.

> And call the result _reasoning_.

This is where bias becomes particularly difficult to see.

Bias runs through much of Kahneman's work.

Not simply as opinions we knowingly hold, but as tendencies in how we notice, interpret, remember, and judge.

Some are easy to recognize once they're pointed out.

Others feel less like bias and more like bedrock.

> _That's obvious._<br>_That's just how I see it._<br>_That's just me._

Except we rarely know exactly when or how that bedrock first formed.

One way to interrupt that is to deliberately introduce another frame.

Kahneman described the value of taking an outside view — looking beyond the story immediately in front of us and asking what becomes visible from a wider set of comparable experiences.

In MNKY Math terms:

Sometimes thinking harder isn't enough. Particularly when the result affirms the first response.

Sometimes we need to alter the lens.

-> Change where we're looking from. <br>-> Question why we're looking.<br>-> Actively seek what might contradict what we already see.

---
## The Feeling Isn't the Interpretation

This distinction matters.

The _feeling_ may be what you notice most.

- Tension
- Curiosity
- Relief
- Unease
- Excitement
- Irritation

But the feeling isn't necessarily the interpretation itself.

It may be one of its expressions. 

Something triggered from inside:

- this matters
- this is familiar
- this might be useful
- this might be dangerous
- pay attention
- move closer
- move away

You may never become consciously aware of everything that contributed to that response.

You simply experience what becomes available to you.

And because it arose inside you...

it often feels naturally like you.

Not a bias. Just you.

---
## Your Baseline Isn't Everyone's Baseline

This helps explain something familiar.

Bias often doesn't feel biased from the inside. It feels like baseline.

Two people can encounter the same thing...<br>and have completely different reactions.

One person hears a song and turns it up.<br>Another reaches for skip.

One person sees a roller coaster and feels excitement.<br>Another feels dread.

One person experiences a rule as helpful structure.<br>Another experiences the same rule as control.

We share much of the same biological machinery.

We learn.<br>We anticipate.<br>We build associations.<br>We respond to reward.<br>We respond to threat.

But we don't share the same histories.

Or expectations.<br>Or sensitivities.<br>Or learned associations.<br>Or present conditions.

The machinery may be similar.

The calibration isn't.

> **Patterned does not mean identical.**

---
## Same Expression. Different Interior Story.

Variation also works in the other direction.

> Two people sit silently in the same meeting.

From the outside:  silence

From the inside?

One may be afraid to disagree.<br>One may have nothing to add.<br>One may be carefully listening.<br>One may be bored or distracted.<br>One may already have checked out.

Same visible expression.

> Very different interior events.

This creates a problem whenever systems try to make sense of people.

Because systems can usually observe the expression.

They cannot directly observe the interpretation that produced it.

They see:

- a click
- a pause
- silence
- a purchase
- a departure
- a return

Then they infer what those expressions mean.

Sometimes the inference is useful.<br>Sometimes it's completely wrong.

---
## Why This Matters

When we look only at visible behavior, it's easy to mistake visible expression for explanation.

-> They clicked because they wanted it.<br>-> They stayed because they were engaged.<br>-> They didn't speak because they agreed.<br>-> They left because they weren't interested.

Maybe.

But something happened between the condition...<br>and what became visible.

Each interior system encountered what was happening.

It interpreted.<br>It responded.

And only part of that process may ever become available to conscious explanation.

Which means understanding behavior requires more than asking: **What did they do?**

It also invites another question: **_What might have happened inside them before they did it?_**

---
## The interpretation to hang with

Sometimes thought isn't where interpretation begins.

It's where we may finally become aware enough to examine our own response... and then to recognize that someone else's may have begun from a different baseline.

The feeling may arrive first.<br>The explanation may arrive later.

And what everyone then gets to see...

is your expression, my expression, and every other available expression.

Not because people are irrational.

Because biology doesn't wait for conscious interpretation.
