# Why Pressure Changes Behavior

September marks the end of summer. 

September also marks the arrival of...

> **Pumpkin Spice season.**

Coffee. Donuts. Muffins. Ice cream. Pancakes. Whatever someone has figured out how to pumpkin-spice this year.

It probably doesn't feel like pressure.

At first, it may feel almost like the opposite.

> **_Something you missed is back._** 

But "_back_" carries another piece of information with it.

> **It won't be here forever.**

That's part of what makes limited-time offers (LTOs) so useful to restaurants and retailers. The system doesn't have to tell you to buy something.

It can simply change the conditions around when you can buy it.

_"Now"_ quietly becomes a little more consequential than later.

➖

We do something similar without a restaurant or retailer involved.

Holidays are limited-time events too.

Thanksgiving occupies a place on the calendar. So does Christmas. A birthday. An anniversary. New Year's Eve. 

_You can eat turkey in March._<br>_You can give someone a present in June._<br>_You can tell someone you love them on an otherwise ordinary Tuesday._

But the date changes something.

> There is a window.<br>
> And windows create consequences.

Travel has to happen on-time. The gift has to arrive by then. The meal has to be ready by then. The person you care about may notice whether you remembered — or didn't.

The calendar hasn't forced you to do anything.

But suddenly when you act matters.

---
## Where Consequence Grows, Pressure Often Follows

Pressure doesn't always feel uncomfortable.

Sometimes it feels like anticipation.

Excitement.<br>Opportunity.<br>The possibility of missing out.

What these experiences can have in common is _consequence_.

Something about acting, waiting, choosing differently, or doing nothing now matters more than it did before.

That doesn't mean every consequence produces pressure. But when a system changes the felt consequence of what happens next, pressure may already be present.

And pressure can do at least two useful things for a system.

### Pressure Can Mobilize

Sometimes the desired response is simply:

> **Do something.**

- The countdown is running
- The sale ends tonight
- The work queue is growing
- The deadline is approaching
- Your flight boards in twelve minutes and you're still walking toward security.

The system doesn't necessarily need to determine exactly what you will do. It only needs to make staying where you are increasingly consequential.

Urgency is especially useful here because it compresses the amount of time available before consequence.

- A Pumpkin Spice drink that's available whenever you want it asks very little of you.
- A Pumpkin Spice drink disappearing next week changes the calculation.

Not dramatically.

Maybe not even consciously.

> But later has become less reliable.<br>Now has become more consequential.

That is mobilizing pressure.

It increases the likelihood that you do the thing the consequence makes feel necessary.

### Pressure Can Also Direct

Other forms of pressure do more than mobilize.

They make one response feel more desirable, appropriate, safe, expected, or necessary than another.

- Everyone else at the table orders dessert.
- Everyone else in the office is still working.
- The person selling you the car leaves the room to “see what the manager can do.”
- Your fitness app reminds you that a 47-day streak ends tonight if you don't act.

Someone you respect says:

> "I'm counting on you."

The pressure isn't simply toward action.

It has direction.

Do this. Stay. Buy. Continue. Agree.

Don't break the streak. Don't disappoint. Don't be the person who behaves differently.

This pressure can come from the system directly.

But systems can often recruit pressures more directly connected to your [[def_interior-systems|interior system]] — belonging, identity, status, responsibility, consistency, fear of loss, or the desire to become someone you hope to be.

Which means the system doesn't always have to manufacture the reason something matters.

Sometimes it only needs to confront or connect with something that already feels like you.

➖

Mobilizing pressure and directional pressure frequently work together.

🔎 A limited-time offer can mobilize through urgency while directing you toward a particular purchase.

🔎 A manager can create urgency around a deadline while invoking professional identity to influence how you respond.

🔎 A game can make time scarce while making advancement, status, or completion increasingly consequential.

Pressure can get you moving.

It can also help determine where you move.

---
## The Window Was Already There

Limited-time offers work because the idea of a limited window isn't new to us.

We already live inside them.

Days begin and end.<br>Weekends arrive and disappear.<br>Seasons change.<br>Birthdays come around once a year.

So do anniversaries, holidays, school years, vacations, sports seasons, harvests, migrations, celebrations, and traditions.

Long before anyone decided pumpkin spice belonged to September, we had already learned something about recurring windows:

>**This happens now.**<br>Then it doesn't.

And, if the pattern continues, eventually it comes back.

That structure becomes familiar.

So when an external system places something inside a window, it doesn't necessarily have to teach us what the window means.

It can borrow from one we already understand.

- Pumpkin Spice borrows from the long-established connection between pumpkins, harvest, and fall.
- A [Shamrock Shake](https://corporate.mcdonalds.com/corpmcd/our-stories/article/shamrock-10fun-facts.html) can become attached to the symbolism and mood surrounding St. Patrick's Day.

The calendar already provides the boundary.

The system only has to place something inside it.

And over repeated returns, the offering can begin acquiring some of the meaning of the window itself.

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem; color: rgb(217, 119, 6);"><strong>-> Fall arrives.<br>= Pumpkin Spice returns.</strong></div>

Eventually, for some people, one can begin signaling the other. Is it fall or is it pumpkin spice season? 

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem; color: rgb(217, 119, 6);"><strong>-> Pumpkin Spice arrives.<br>= Fall returns.</strong></div>

But systems don't have to depend on an existing calendar.

They can create windows of their own.

The [McRib](https://www.mcdonalds.com/us/en-us/product/mcrib.html) doesn't need a holiday. [Taco Bell's Nacho Fries](https://www.tacobell.com/food/deals-and-combos/large-nacho-fries) spent years doing something similar, disappearing and returning before becoming a regular menu item... while today specialty flavors continue to come and go, shifting the limited-time pressure from the product itself to its variations.

It can disappear.<br>Then return.<br>Then disappear again.

Over time, the recurrence itself can become familiar. You may not know exactly _when_ it will return. But you learn that availability is temporary.

The system has created a window without needing a date on the calendar.

And once that structure is familiar, other things can be placed inside it.

- A product can be available only today.
- Only while supplies last.
- Only in certain locations.
- Only to certain customers.
- Only once.

Time isn't the only thing that can be limited.

- Availability can be limited
- Access can be limited
- Frequency can be limited
- Even the likelihood of encountering something can be limited

The _window_ was already there — part of a [[def_consequence-structure|consequence structure]] your interior system has been building, testing, and refining throughout your life.

You already know that some things happen only at certain times.<br>That opportunities can close.<br>That waiting can change what remains available.<br>That something absent today may return — and disappear again.

An external system doesn't necessarily have to teach you any of that.

But it can place something new inside a consequence structure you already understand.

---
## The pressure to hang with

Pressure doesn't always announce itself as pressure.

Sometimes it feels like urgency.<br>Sometimes opportunity.<br>Sometimes responsibility.<br>Sometimes simply wanting something.

So the next time something feels unusually compelling, consider where that feeling is coming from.

**Is it coming from inside you?**

**From the conditions surrounding the thing?**

**Or from some combination of the two?**

And if the answer seems simpler...

_I just like it._<br>_I prefer it._<br>_I enjoy it._

... there may still be another question worth asking:

> **Where did that preference come from?**

Not because every preference was engineered.

And not because enjoying something means you've been manipulated.

But because preferences can grow inside conditions too.

Because if Pumpkin Spice were always available, there could be no return.

And without the return...

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;"><strong>what might happen to the wanting?</strong></div>

And if the wanting changed...

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem;"><strong>what might happen to the preference?</strong></div>
