**“How could they not know that?”**

# ChatGPT

Yes. I think this adds a very important distinction:

**“Who needs the knowledge?” is not a single question.**

There is:

- who the sender believes needs it,
- who the system requires to receive it,
- who the receiver believes needs it,
- and who actually needs it when the consequence finally arrives.

Those can be four different answers.

That makes this much richer than a simple knowledge-distribution problem. It becomes partly an **attention, relevance, and consequence problem**.

Your compliance-training example is almost perfect. The organization says, _you need to know this._ The employee may experience it as, _I need to complete this._ Those are not the same thing. The system can successfully distribute the information and record completion while very little knowledge is actually incorporated.

So we get something like:

**information delivered ≠ knowledge received ≠ knowledge retained ≠ knowledge used**

And perhaps even before all of those:

**information delivered ≠ information experienced as relevant**

That same mechanism shows up in your project-update example, but with higher stakes.

Someone reports drift for six weeks.

The information exists.  
It has been distributed.  
Leadership may even have read it.

But it hasn't crossed whatever threshold makes it feel consequential enough to become action.

Then the consequence changes.

Suddenly the same information becomes:

> Why didn't somebody tell us?

And when shown that somebody did:

> Why didn't somebody make it more urgent?

That second question is fascinating because it reveals an unspoken expectation that the **sender was also responsible for correctly calibrating the receiver's attention**.

The sender wasn't merely expected to communicate the signal. They were expected to somehow make the receiver assign the appropriate weight to it.

That feels very connected to M32/M33:

**signal → interior system → perceived consequence → attention/action**

The project drift didn't suddenly become new information. Its **weight changed**.

That gives _The Fallacy of Knowledge_ another important proposition:

> **Knowledge can be available to someone without becoming consequential to them.**

And maybe an even stronger one:

> **A system can successfully transmit information and still fail to create usable knowledge.**

Because what happens after transmission still depends on the receiver's interior system: what they already know, what else is competing for attention, what they trust, what appears urgent, what they believe matters now, and what consequence they associate with ignoring it.

This also complicates the phrase we just developed:

> **Knowledge can be present in the system and still be functionally absent.**

Now there are at least two ways that can happen.

It can be held by the wrong person.

Or it can reach the right person **without being recognized as something worth acting on**.

And that suggests a very strong section heading someday:

### Knowing isn't the same as caring yet

Maybe not literally that wording, but the distinction is powerful.

The project-update example could eventually give the essay one of its best questions:

> When someone says, “Why didn't anyone tell me?”, are they asking whether the information was available...
> 
> **or whether the information ever became important enough to them to notice?**

And I think your “why didn't someone make it more urgent?” observation exposes one more uncomfortable systems issue: if everything has to be made urgent before it receives attention, **urgency itself becomes part of the communication protocol**. Then everyone learns to escalate, color things red, bold the subject line, add executives, mark messages HIGH PRIORITY, and manufacture consequence just to get ordinary information seen.

Eventually the system trains people to shout.

That feels like an entire branch of _The Fallacy of Knowledge_ by itself.
