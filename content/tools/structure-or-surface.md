# Surface or Structure

**Surface or Structure** is a diagnostic for examining whether the visible evidence of something is supported by the system underneath it.

Use this Tool when a system claims to value, practice, protect, or produce something important.

That might be:

- learning
- accountability
- customer focus
- employee engagement
- psychological safety
- agility
- inclusion
- empowerment
- safety
- quality

The Tool does not assume that surface is false or unimportant.

Surface helps people recognize, remember, communicate, and coordinate around what matters.

But visible evidence is not proof that the conditions required to make the evidence true still exist.

Surface may remain after the structure has weakened, changed, or disappeared.

The central question is:

> **What needs to be true for the surface to be true?**

That question moves the work from appearance toward design.

---

## When to use this Tool

Surface or Structure may be useful when:

- a stated value does not match people’s experience
- a ritual continues without producing its intended effect
- a metric looks healthy while outcomes are weakening
- a policy exists but is difficult to rely on
- training has occurred without an observable change in behavior
- leaders and participants describe the same system differently
- a practice appears successful but depends on informal work people cannot see
- a system looks aligned while repeatedly producing contradiction, avoidance, or frustration

It can also be used before adopting something new.

A team considering a new ritual, metric, framework, or program can use the Tool to ask what supporting structure would need to exist before the visible practice is introduced.

---

## Before you begin

Choose one specific claim, practice, signal, or outcome to examine.

Try not to diagnose an entire organization at once.

Instead of examining whether the organization supports learning, begin with something more bounded:

- how one team responds when someone reports a mistake
- what happens during one recurring retrospective
- how one customer measure is used in decisions
- whether one employee feedback process produces a response
- what authority accompanies one role described as empowered

Define the part of the system you are examining and the period of time you are considering.

Different areas of the same system may have different structures.

A surface may be supported in one team, contradicted in another, and dependent on informal work somewhere else.

---

## A quick diagnostic

### 1. Name the claim

What does the system say it values, practices, protects, or produces?

State the claim as plainly as possible.

> We value learning.  
> Employees are empowered.  
> Customer experience drives our decisions.  
> This meeting improves coordination.  
> This metric represents quality.

The claim is the thing the surface appears to make visible.

---

### 2. Name the intended meaning

What is the claim, practice, or signal meant to produce?

A ritual, metric, title, policy, or value statement usually exists to serve something beyond itself.

Ask:

- What outcome was this intended to support?
- What behavior was it meant to encourage?
- What risk was it meant to reduce?
- What meaning was it supposed to protect?
- What should people be able to do or rely on because it exists?

A daily meeting may be intended to improve coordination.

A training program may be intended to change behavior.

A dashboard may be intended to focus attention.

A feedback survey may be intended to help the system learn.

Naming the intended meaning gives you something more useful to examine than whether the visible practice merely exists.

---

### 3. Identify the surface

What can people see, hear, name, point to, repeat, attend, display, or measure?

Surface may include:

- language
- values
- metrics
- dashboards
- meetings
- rituals
- policies
- training
- titles
- tools
- roles
- symbols
- promises
- ceremonies
- public statements

Ask:

- What makes the claim recognizable?
- What visible evidence suggests it may be true?
- What would someone point to as proof?
- Which parts of the surface receive the most attention?
- Which parts are measured, reported, or reviewed?

Surface is evidence worth examining.

It is not yet the conclusion.

---

### 4. Investigate the structure

What conditions make the claim likely, supported, repeatable, consequential, or durable?

Look beneath the visible evidence.

Structure may live in:

- incentives
- consequences
- decision rights
- authority
- resources
- time
- staffing
- workflows
- processes
- constraints
- measurements
- feedback loops
- repeated practice
- leadership behavior
- participant expectations
- what becomes safe or risky
- what becomes easy or costly

Ask:

- What behavior does the system reward?
- What behavior does it punish or make difficult?
- Who can make decisions?
- Who carries the consequences?
- What happens when the visible practice conflicts with pressure, urgency, or targets?
- What happens when someone acts according to the stated value?
- What happens when someone does not?
- What informal work is required to keep the surface functioning?

Structure is not only what was formally designed.

It also includes what participants have had to build, adapt, avoid, or work around to keep the system operating.

---

### 5. Compare what is displayed with what is produced

Place the surface and structure beside one another.

Ask:

- Where does the structure support the surface?
- Where does the surface exceed the structure?
- Where does the structure contradict the surface?
- Where has the surface remained after the structure changed?
- Where does useful structure exist but remain difficult to see?
- What behavior and outcomes are actually being produced?

Then ask the MNKY Math question:

> **What is the system actually training people to do?**

The answer may not match what the system intended.

It may not match what leaders believe is happening.

It may also differ across roles, locations, teams, or moments of pressure.

---

### 6. Ask what needs to become true

The purpose of this Tool is not only to expose contradiction.

It is to help identify what the system would need in order to make its visible claims more reliable.

Ask:

- What structure is missing?
- What existing structure needs to be strengthened?
- What contradiction needs to be removed?
- What behavior needs protection?
- What authority, time, information, or resources are required?
- What should stop being rewarded?
- What surface should change because the system underneath has changed?
- What useful structure deserves to become more visible?
- What is the smallest change that would make the surface more true?

The answer may involve strengthening the structure.

It may involve changing the surface.

Sometimes it requires both.

---

## Possible patterns

Surface and structure do not always produce a simple yes-or-no finding.

You may discover several different relationships.

### Supported surface

The visible signal is backed by conditions that make it reliable.

What the system says, shows, and reinforces generally point in the same direction.

### Unsupported surface

The visible practice exists, but little underneath sustains its intended meaning.

The system has the language, ritual, metric, or policy without the conditions required to make it consequential.

### Contradictory structure

The system displays one value while its incentives, decisions, consequences, or constraints train another behavior.

The surface says one thing.

Participation inside the system requires something else.

### Hidden structure

A useful practice is real and repeatable but poorly named, recognized, or coordinated around.

People may be producing the desired outcome through informal structures that the wider system does not see or protect.

### Stale surface

The visible evidence remains after the system underneath has changed.

A metric, meeting, role, process, or promise may still be present even though it no longer represents how the system operates.

More than one pattern may be present at the same time.

The purpose is not to assign the system a permanent label.

The purpose is to make the relationship easier to see and discuss.

---

## Using the Tool on your own

When working individually:

- separate what you observed from what you inferred
- record specific examples rather than relying only on impressions
- note where you have direct experience and where you do not
- consider how the system may look from another role
- distinguish the intended design from the effect being produced
- revisit the diagnostic after gathering additional evidence

Your experience inside the system matters.

But no single participant can see the entire system from one position.

Treat your first pass as an investigation, not a verdict.

---

## Using the Tool with a group

When using Surface or Structure with others, begin with a bounded claim or practice rather than a judgment about the entire system.

Ask participants to bring observations and examples.

Avoid beginning with:

> This organization does not value learning.

Begin with:

> What happens after someone reports a mistake?

That shift makes the system easier to examine without requiring immediate agreement about motives.

A facilitator can help the group:

- distinguish evidence from interpretation
- make room for differences across roles
- identify where participants experience different consequences
- record both supporting and contradictory structure
- prevent the discussion from becoming an evaluation of individual character
- keep the focus on what the system makes likely

Disagreement can be useful evidence.

Different experiences may reveal that the same surface is supported for some participants and contradicted for others.

The goal is not to force a shared score.

The goal is to create a clearer shared picture of the system.

---

## Do not turn the Tool into another surface

Completing the diagnostic does not change the system.

A workshop, worksheet, or discussion can itself become surface if nothing underneath changes.

The Tool has done useful work when it helps people:

- notice a relationship they could not previously name
- gather better evidence
- discuss contradiction more clearly
- protect structure that is already working
- reconsider a misleading surface
- change a condition that shapes behavior
- test whether the change produces a different result

The completed exercise is not the outcome.

What the system does differently afterward is part of the outcome.
