# Increasing Resolution: The Evolution of Advertising Measurement

For as long as businesses have tried to influence what people do, they have faced the same basic problem: **How do you connect an action taken by the business with a behavior that happens later?**

An advertisement appears.<br>A coupon is distributed.<br>A promotion launches.<br>An influencer mentions you.

Someone eventually visits, returns, buys, clicks, or does nothing at all. 

The distance between these two moments has always been difficult to see clearly. Much of the history of advertising measurement is the history of trying to reduce that distance — increasing the resolution and speed with which businesses can observe what happened between action and response.

---
## Significant Historical Milestones

➖ **Before mass markets — proximity**

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem; margin-bottom: 2.1rem;">Producer, seller, and buyer were often much closer together. Feedback could be direct and local.

_Industrial production and expanding distribution increasingly separated the producer from the individual buyer. Mass advertising helped manufacturers reach those buyers. But that distance created a new problem: how could a manufacturer know which advertising had actually worked?_</div>

➖ **Late 1800s — measurable response**

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem; margin-bottom: 2.1rem;">Mass-produced branded goods and mass advertising expand. Coupons, mail offers, order forms, and similar devices begin leaving countable responses. Coca-Cola’s large-scale coupon campaigns illustrate this early shift toward promotions that could produce a visible, countable response.</div>

➖ **Early 1900s — source identification**

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem; margin-bottom: 2.1rem;">Keyed coupons/order forms allow advertisers to ask not merely did somebody respond? But also, which advertisement did they respond to?

⏵ Broadcast era — _audience without individual traceability_

Radio and television enormously expand reach, while direct connection between exposure and purchase becomes harder. Audience measurement tells advertisers who probably encountered something; sales tell them what happened afterward. Connecting the two remains difficult.</div>
➖ **1950s–1980s — modeling effects**

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem; margin-bottom: 2.1rem;">Statistical/econometric approaches develop to estimate relationships among advertising, promotions, sales, seasonality, and other variables. Marketing-mix modeling eventually becomes an important approach for channels where individual-level tracking isn't possible.

⏵ Direct marketing, barcodes, loyalty systems — _individual traces grow richer_

Responses, purchases, households, products, promotions, and eventually customer identities become increasingly linkable. Coupon clearinghouses and UPC scanning are useful milestones here; Nielsen established a coupon clearinghouse in 1957, and UPC scanning appeared in retail in 1974.</div>

➖ **1990s–2000s — the click changes expectations**

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem; margin-bottom: 2.1rem;">Digital advertising can increasingly connect impression → click → visit → action, producing the feeling that attribution can become deterministic rather than inferred.</div>

➖ **2010s — multi-touch everything**

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem; margin-bottom: 2.1rem;">Multiple devices, channels, platforms, retargeting, identity graphs, and customer data make the trace richer while simultaneously making the causal question harder: which touch actually mattered?</div>

➖ **2020s — the attribution paradox**

<div style="margin-left: 1.4rem; margin-bottom: 2.1rem;">We have vastly more data and vastly more sophisticated models, but privacy restrictions, disappearing identifiers, fragmented platforms, and increasingly complex journeys have renewed interest in aggregate experimentation and MMM. More traces have not eliminated the fundamental problem of causation.</div>

---
## What Advertisers Can Connect Today

Today's advertising systems can draw from a much richer collection of signals than the early coupon ever provided. Not every advertiser has access to all of them, and privacy rules, platforms, permissions, and technical architecture determine what can actually be used. But together they illustrate how dramatically measurement resolution has increased.

- **Cookies**: recognize a browser or session across interactions and, depending on the cookie, over time.
- **Tracking pixels**: create a record that an event such as a page view, email open, or conversion occurred.
- **Loyalty IDs**: connect transactions and other activity to a persistent customer relationship.
- **Advertising IDs**: provide mobile devices with pseudonymous identifiers that can support advertising measurement and targeting.
- **Device and browser fingerprints**: infer a distinctive signature from combinations of device or browser characteristics rather than relying on a single issued identifier.
- **Identity graphs**: connect multiple identifiers, devices, accounts, and signals into an inferred representation of the same person or household.
- **Geolocation**: add movement and place to other behavioral traces.
- **Re-identification and de-identification**: explore when supposedly anonymous or pseudonymous data can — or cannot — be associated again with a person.
