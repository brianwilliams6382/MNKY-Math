## Consequence Structure

**Consequence structures are learned relationships within an [[def_interior-systems|interior system]] connecting conditions, actions, timing, and what tends to happen next.**

Throughout life, we repeatedly encounter patterns such as:

- If I wait, this may no longer be available.
- If I do this, something else tends to happen.
- If I don't respond, there may be a cost.
- Some opportunities return.
- Some disappear.
- Certain actions are expected in certain settings.
- Particular signals often precede particular outcomes.

Through repeated experience, memory, observation, social learning, and direct consequence, these relationships can become familiar enough that a person does not need to consciously reconstruct them each time.

The present condition can be interpreted partly through what prior experience has taught the interior system tends to follow.

## The Structure Does Not Determine the Response

A consequence structure is not a rule guaranteeing behavior.

Two people can encounter the same condition while bringing different histories, expectations, resources, priorities, identities, and learned associations to it.

The same person may also respond differently as circumstances change.

A consequence structure helps make a condition **legible**.

It gives the interior system something from prior experience to use when interpreting what might happen next.

## Systems Can Use Structures They Didn't Create

An external system does not always need to teach a participant an entirely new relationship between action and consequence.

It can connect a new condition to a consequence structure the participant already understands.

A limited-time offer can rely on an already familiar relationship:

**availability → waiting → possible loss of opportunity**

A workplace deadline can rely on another:

**time → completion → expectation or consequence**

A loyalty streak might connect:

**continued participation → accumulated progress → breaking the sequence means losing something already built**

The system may create the immediate condition without creating the underlying consequence structure that makes the condition meaningful.

## Consequence Structures and Bananas

A consequence structure becomes especially powerful when what may happen next involves something a person already values, needs, wants, protects, or fears losing.

MNKY Math refers to these things as [[mm_bananas|bananas]].

The banana provides the stake.

The consequence structure helps make the relationship legible:

- **If I do this, I may gain or protect the banana.**<br><br>
- **If I do not, I may lose it, delay it, reduce access to it, or increase the cost of getting it.**

A system can introduce a new condition while relying on a familiar consequence structure and an already meaningful banana.

This is one way systems can create incentive, pressure, or avoidance without explicitly instructing someone how to respond.

## Consequence Structures Can Change

They are learned, not fixed.

New experience can reinforce an existing structure, modify it, or make an old relationship less useful.

Something repeatedly available may stop feeling scarce.

A deadline repeatedly extended may stop producing the same urgency.

A person who learns they can safely decline a social expectation may experience less pressure the next time it appears.

What the interior system expects can change because what happened next changed.

## Plain Language

A consequence structure is part of what lets you encounter something happening now and have your interior system already carry some sense of:

**If this... then possibly that.**

Within a person's interior system, the logic tends to work something like this:

1. **I expect X may lead to Y.**
2. **I act. Something happens.**
3. **I compare what happened with what I expected.**
4. **That experience strengthens, weakens, or complicates the expectation.**
5. **The interior system updates accordingly.**

## In Practice

Consider the banana = **status**

A person may have learned several consequence structures around challenging a prevailing view:

**challenge the prevailing view → social risk → possible status loss**

But they may also have learned:

**challenge the prevailing view → social acceptance → possible status gain**

And choosing not to challenge can carry its own learned relationships:

**challenge nothing → social conformity → possible status preservation**

or:

**challenge nothing → reduced visibility → possible status stagnation**

or:

**challenge nothing → missed opportunity → possible status loss**

None of these relationships guarantees what a person will do — **_the structure does not determine the response_**. 

They make the moment legible. It helps explain, for example, why two people in the same meeting can experience the exact same invitation to speak as entirely different conditions.

- Someone who has repeatedly been punished for challenging authority may experience speaking up as carrying immediate social risk.

- Someone whose experience has taught them that thoughtful challenge earns respect may interpret the same moment as an opportunity for status gain.

- Another person may have learned that staying quiet preserves belonging, predictability, or position.

The external condition may be the same, while prior or analogous experience leads each person's interior system to expect something different may happen next.

### When There Is No Direct Reference

A person does not need to have experienced the exact situation before.

When there is no direct prior reference, the interior system can draw from other learned relationships and present conditions to form a provisional expectation.

Someone entering a new team, for example, may not know what happens when disagreement is voiced there.

But they may still bring:

- prior experiences with authority or conflict,
- beliefs about what someone in their role should do,
- concern for belonging or status,
- confidence in their own judgment,
- observations of how others are treated, and
- signals about what appears safe or rewarded.

These do not yet constitute a learned consequence structure specific to the new team.

They help the person infer what may happen.

The response that follows — and what actually happens next — can then reinforce, modify, or begin forming a consequence structure for future encounters.

### When Denial Enters the Frame

[[def_denial|Denial]] can interfere with how a consequence structure updates.

A consequence may occur without being fully acknowledged, connected to the action that preceded it, or allowed to revise what the interior system expects may happen next.

This can allow an existing consequence structure to persist even as contradictory evidence accumulates. For a deeper look see [[ref_denial-and-consequence-structures|Denial and Consequence Structures]].

## Why This Matters

Making consequence structures visible does not tell us what someone will do.

It helps us ask why a particular response may feel reasonable from inside that person's position.

For someone participating in a system, this can make an important distinction visible:

**What is happening now?**

and

**What have I learned or inferred may happen next?**

Those are not always the same thing.

A consequence structure may still accurately describe the system.

It may also reflect an earlier environment, a different authority relationship, an outdated expectation, or a pattern that has never been tested in the present setting.

Making the structure visible creates an opportunity to examine it rather than simply act through it.

For someone designing or managing a system, consequence structures help explain why the same condition can produce very different responses. 

A request to speak openly may look equally available to everyone.<br>_It may not feel equally safe._ 

A deadline may appear equally clear.<br>_It may not carry the same anticipated consequence._ 

A reward may look equally valuable.<br>_It may not connect to the same banana._ 

Seeing these differences can help designers ask better questions: 

- What consequences are people learning to expect here? 
- What signals are reinforcing those expectations? 
- What behavior appears rational from inside those expectations? 
- Are we unintentionally rewarding, punishing, or discouraging something different from what we intend? 
- What would have to change for a different response to become reasonable?

Consequence structures help move the question from **“Why did this person do that?”** toward **_“What did the person have reason to expect would happen next?”_**

## Related Concepts

These concepts overlap, but describe different parts of the interaction:

- **[[def_interior-systems|Interior systems]]** describe the biological, emotional, cognitive, and learned processes participating in interpretation and response.
- **[[def_routine|Routine]]** provides recurring structure through which expectations and baselines can develop.
- **[[when-repeating-becomes-routine|Baseline]]** describes what has become expected under familiar conditions.
- **Consequence structures** connect conditions and possible actions with what experience suggests may follow.
- **[[how-systems-put-pressure-to-work|Pressure]]** can emerge when a condition makes one of those possible consequences feel more immediate or consequential.
- **[[def_habit|Habit]]** describes a learned tendency for familiar cues or conditions to call up a recurring response with less conscious deliberation.
- **[[def_denial|Denial]]** can interfere with how consequence structures are interpreted and updated, allowing a learned expectation to persist even when new experience might otherwise weaken or complicate it.

## References

Consequence structures overlap with several established frameworks that examine how people learn relationships between actions, outcomes, rewards, values, and observed behavior.

They are not identical to the MNKY Math concept, but they help locate it within a larger body of thinking.

- **[[expectancy-theory|Expectancy Theory]]** — examines how motivation is shaped by expectations that effort will lead to performance, performance will lead to outcomes, and those outcomes will carry value. Its emphasis on *what leads to what* closely overlaps with the predictive logic inside consequence structures.

- **[[means-end-chain-theory|Means-End Chain Theory]]** — connects attributes to consequences and consequences to personal values. It is especially relevant to the relationship between external conditions, anticipated consequences, and what MNKY Math calls bananas.

- **Operant Conditioning and Reinforcement Learning** — examine how consequences following behavior can increase or decrease the likelihood of future behavior. These help explain one way consequence structures can be learned and reinforced through experience.

- **Incentive Salience** — examines how learned cues associated with rewards can acquire motivational importance. This is relevant when a present signal activates an already meaningful learned relationship inside an interior system.

- **Revealed Preference** — uses observed choices as evidence about what people value or prefer. It is relevant to the reverse problem: what, if anything, can behavior tell us about the consequence structures or bananas operating underneath it?

- **Inverse Reinforcement Learning and Bayesian Inverse Planning** — computational approaches that infer likely goals, rewards, beliefs, or intentions from observed behavior. These are especially relevant to the question of how a system might infer possible consequence structures from a person's expression or response.

Together, these frameworks suggest that consequence structures are not an isolated idea: they sit near established work on expectation, learning, value, motivation, and inference.

MNKY Math uses the concept to focus specifically on how those learned relationships become part of the interior system a person brings into participation.
