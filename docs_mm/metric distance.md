Yes — this is extremely revealing, and I think you’re naming the deeper implication correctly.

The Lead Edge hierarchy is not just a hierarchy of **reported information**. It is a hierarchy of **distance from economic reality**.

At the top, the company can speak directly about the engine:

> Cash profits  
> Adjusted profits  
> Gross profits  
> Revenue

Then, as the engine gets weaker or less proven, the conversation moves outward:

> GMV  
> Monthly active users  
> Subscribers  
> Downloads  
> Pageviews  
> Awards / “Best Place to Work”

The further down the list you go, the more the conversation shifts from **value creation** to **value implication**.

That’s the phrase I’d hold onto.

## Value creation vs. value implication

Revenue says:

> Someone paid us.

Profit says:

> We created value and captured enough of it to sustain the business.

GMV says:

> Value may be moving through our system.

Monthly active users says:

> People are present.

Downloads says:

> People were curious enough to try.

Pageviews says:

> Attention occurred.

Awards say:

> Someone external signaled approval.

None of those lower metrics are worthless. But they are progressively more distant from the core question:

> Is this system producing durable value?

That maps beautifully to MNKY Math.

## The MNKY Math version

Lead Edge is effectively saying:

> The weaker the business engine, the more abstract the metric becomes.

MNKY Math might say:

> The weaker the connection between metric and outcome, the more the system depends on narrative to make the metric feel meaningful.

That is a major insight.

Because the problem is not that pageviews, downloads, users, or awards are fake. The problem is that they can become **stand-ins** for the thing people actually want to know.

And this happens far beyond startups.

## This applies to almost every system

In business:

> We don’t have profit, so we talk about revenue.  
> We don’t have revenue, so we talk about users.  
> We don’t have users, so we talk about downloads.  
> We don’t have downloads, so we talk about visibility.

In operations:

> We don’t have improved outcomes, so we talk about completed tasks.  
> We don’t have completed tasks, so we talk about meetings held.  
> We don’t have meetings held, so we talk about alignment efforts.

In employee experience:

> We don’t have trust, so we talk about engagement scores.  
> We don’t have engagement, so we talk about survey participation.  
> We don’t have participation, so we talk about culture initiatives.

In product:

> We don’t have retention, so we talk about activation.  
> We don’t have activation, so we talk about signups.  
> We don’t have signups, so we talk about impressions.

This is where MNKY Math has a strong voice. It can ask:

> How far is the reported metric from the real outcome?

That could become a canonical diagnostic question.

## Possible MNKY Math lens: Metric Distance

This might be worth naming as a reusable lens:

> **Metric Distance**  
> The distance between the number being reported and the outcome the number is being used to imply.

Or:

> **Outcome Distance**  
> The gap between what is being measured and what the system is claiming that measurement represents.

The Lead Edge hierarchy is basically a “metric distance ladder.” The further down you go, the more interpretation is required. The more interpretation is required, the more room there is for narrative, theater, selective framing, or self-deception.

## Why this matters for Home/About

This gives MNKY Math a sharper way to describe its purpose.

Not:

> We critique metrics.

But:

> We examine the distance between metrics and outcomes.

That is much more precise.

Possible About language:

> Metrics are necessary. But every metric sits at some distance from the outcome it is used to represent. The greater that distance becomes, the more room there is for distortion: incentives shift, behavior adapts, narratives fill the gap, and systems begin optimizing for signals instead of substance.

That feels very MNKY Math.

## The “engine” metaphor is strong

Your wording — “the further from the engine the metric that is held up is” — is excellent.

You could develop this into a durable model:

**The Engine**  
The actual value-producing mechanism.

**The Exhaust**  
Secondary signals produced by the engine.

**The Smoke**  
Weak signals that suggest something might be happening.

**The Spotlight**  
Narrative signals used to attract belief when the engine is not yet visible.

That might be too stylized for the Home page, but as a future article or framework, it has legs.

A cleaner version:

> The closer a metric is to the engine, the less narrative it needs.  
> The farther it gets from the engine, the more narrative it requires.

That line is strong enough to become an article thesis.

## This also helps distinguish MNKY Math from financial writing

CJ / Lead Edge are focused on financial truth:

> Is this metric close enough to money to matter?

MNKY Math can broaden that:

> Is this metric close enough to the outcome to be trusted?

That applies to revenue, but also to learning, safety, productivity, connection, health, culture, retention, service quality, customer value, and human agency.

So MNKY Math’s domain is wider. It studies how systems use signals to claim progress — and how those signals can drift away from the thing they were supposed to represent.

## This feels like a canonical MNKY Math principle

A possible formulation:

> **The further a metric sits from the outcome, the more carefully we should examine the behavior it creates.**

Or sharper:

> **The farther the metric is from the engine, the more likely the system is managing belief instead of producing value.**

That second one has more edge. It may be article-worthy.
