Yes — this is a very useful counterpoint, and I think it actually clarifies MNKY Math’s voice rather than threatening it.

The CJ Gustafson article is basically saying: **be precise about the money math.** Don’t call interest revenue. Don’t call GMV revenue. Don’t call bookings ARR. The article walks through levels of “is it revenue?” from weak signals like non-binding interest, to GMV, to bookings/CARR, to transactional revenue, and finally true recurring revenue. It is funny, sharp, operator-friendly, and unapologetically oriented around financial truth.

MNKY Math does not need to argue against that.

In fact, MNKY Math can stand beside it and say:

> Yes. The money math matters.  
> But money math is not the only math operating in the system.

That may be the key.

## The contrast is not “money vs. humanity”

I don’t think MNKY Math should position itself as the humane antidote to hard-nosed financial thinking. That would make MNKY Math sound soft, moralizing, or anti-business.

The better distinction is:

> Financial metrics tell you what the system is producing.  
> MNKY Math asks what the system is becoming in order to produce it.

That gives MNKY Math a legitimate voice in the “show me the money” world because you are not rejecting money. You are asking whether the path to money is creating hidden fragility, behavioral distortion, cultural debt, customer resentment, employee exhaustion, metric theater, or second-order costs that will eventually show up somewhere else.

CJ’s piece is concerned with **metric truth**.

MNKY Math is concerned with **system truth**.

Those are complementary.

## CJ is asking: “Is it actually revenue?”

That is a strong diagnostic question.

MNKY Math can ask parallel questions:

> Is it actually performance?  
> Is it actually productivity?  
> Is it actually alignment?  
> Is it actually retention?  
> Is it actually loyalty?  
> Is it actually engagement?  
> Is it actually trust?  
> Is it actually agency?  
> Is it actually an outcome — or just a metric wearing the costume of one?

That last phrase connects directly to the “Is It Cake?” metaphor. CJ’s article shows that some reported revenue is not really revenue. MNKY Math can show that some reported success is not really success.

That is a very strong positioning insight for the Home/About pages.

## The business case for MNKY Math is not “be nicer”

The business case is:

> When metrics become the goal, systems learn how to satisfy the metric — sometimes at the expense of the outcome the metric was supposed to represent.

That is not anti-capitalist. It is operationally serious.

A company can absolutely “print money” for a while through pricing power, market position, habit, lock-in, switching costs, or extraction. But MNKY Math’s question is:

> What else is the system spending to produce that money?

It might be spending trust.  
It might be spending employee discretion.  
It might be spending brand goodwill.  
It might be spending future optionality.  
It might be spending customer patience.  
It might be spending resilience.

The financials may not show that immediately. But the system is still absorbing the cost.

## The Netflix example is a perfect MNKY Math doorway

Your Netflix example gets right to the heart of it.

The “show me the money” math says:

> Raise price by $2. Churn remains tolerable. Revenue increases. Good decision.

MNKY Math asks:

> What did the customer relationship learn from that decision?

Because customer behavior may say “I stayed,” while customer sentiment says “I trust you less.” The metric captures retention. It may not capture resentment. It may not capture declining goodwill. It may not capture the moment when staying becomes inertia rather than loyalty.

That is the MNKY Math opening:

> The system can measure whether people leave before it understands why they stay.

That sentence feels very close to the Home/About thesis.

## Where MNKY Math has a voice

MNKY Math has a voice in the money world because businesses already know the limits of surface-level metrics. They just often lack language for the second-order effects.

CJ’s piece exposes one kind of metric inflation:

> Calling something revenue before it is truly revenue.

MNKY Math exposes another kind:

> Calling something success before the system has actually improved.

That is the lane.

You are not saying, “Stop caring about money.”

You are saying:

> Be careful when the metric starts replacing the reality it was designed to represent.

Or, more commercially:

> MNKY Math studies the gap between measured performance and actual system health.

That is credible. That is useful. And that belongs on the site.

CJ’s article makes money legible.

MNKY Math can make the system around the money legible.

The “show me the money” crowd may not care about human-centered language by itself, but they absolutely care when hidden system costs become execution risk, retention risk, customer risk, reputation risk, or growth risk.

So MNKY Math’s voice in that space is not:
